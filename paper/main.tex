\section{Abstract}

This study investigates the profitability of a statistical arbitrage strategy applied to binary outcome contracts on the Kalshi Exchange, focusing on end-of-year S\&P 500 price buckets. The objective is to assess whether derivatives-implied probability distributions can identify mispriced contracts in Kalshi's event markets.

Probability mass functions are derived from SPX options using two approaches: a risk-neutral density (RND) extracted via the Breeden--Litzenberger method applied to a smoothed implied-volatility surface, and a Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) benchmark using at-the-money implied volatility. Cleaned and interpolated option-chain data construct these distributions daily; they are compared to Kalshi's order-book-implied probabilities to build long--short portfolios of under- and over-priced contracts. To keep risk bounded on a small capital base, exposure is capped at a single lot per bucket (no pyramiding). Performance is benchmarked against the SPX index using Sharpe ratio, drawdowns, volatility, and alpha/beta.

Backtesting from 2022 to 2024 shows that, under risk-managed position sizing and requiring both markets to be genuinely live before a new position is opened, the option-implied signals produce small but generally positive, low-drawdown, near market-neutral returns, with the Geometric Brownian Motion benchmark attaining the highest single Sharpe ratio (1.41 in 2022) while the Breeden--Litzenberger method is the only one profitable in every year tested. An independent, model-free check --- replicating each Kalshi bucket with an SPX iron condor --- finds that roughly one quarter of bucket quotes exhibit a cross-market dislocation larger than the replication cost, corroborating the density-based signal. Execution limitations, low liquidity, and missing end-of-year 2024 data constrain realized performance. Across all years the strategies show low correlation to SPX.

These findings suggest option-implied information can help detect pricing inefficiencies in prediction markets, though a realistic, risk-controlled implementation yields modest rather than dramatic returns. Ongoing improvements focus on hedged execution, expanded contract coverage, and fee-free limit-order modeling.

\section{Introduction to Kalshi}
Kalshi is a federally regulated exchange that allows traders to speculate on real-world events through the trading of ``event contracts.'' These contracts are priced according to the collective expectations of the market of an event occurring, providing a platform that blends financial markets with prediction markets. Kalshi's offerings span a wide range of topics, from traditional economic indicators to unconventional events such as weather and U.S. presidential election outcomes. Operating under the oversight of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), Kalshi provides a regulated framework for traders.

Event contracts are structured similarly to binary options, with a payout of 100 cents if the predicted event occurs and 0 cents otherwise. For example, if the market estimates a 40\% chance that the S\&P 500 Index (SPX) will close within a specific range---say, \$5250 to \$5500---on a given day, the corresponding contract is priced at 40 cents. Upon expiration, the contract settles at its payoff value based on the event outcome. The focus of this project is Kalshi contracts tied to the year-end closing price of the S\&P 500, which define payout ``buckets'' for specific \$200-wide price ranges (e.g., \$6000--\$6199.99).

Several features of the Kalshi exchange should be considered. Relative to standardized exchanges, Kalshi markets operate 24/7, so SPX-related trades can be placed after-hours. Kalshi offers an open order-book market with market makers supplying liquidity when possible. Mark-to-market is net of fees; there are no fees for limit orders, but market-order fees are $\lceil 0.035\cdot C\cdot p\cdot (1-p) \rceil$ cents, where $C$ is the contract size and $p$ the contract price. Because contract prices are bounded in $(0,1)$ regardless of horizon, Kalshi pays a 3.75\% APY on cash \emph{and} on all open positions, effectively leveraging a fully-invested portfolio. As shown later, this feature materially affects strategy returns.

\section{Data Preprocessing}
This study draws on two datasets: SPXW option-chain data obtained via Refinitiv through Hanlon Labs, and Kalshi event-contract order-book data accessed through Kalshi's Python API. The raw options dataset contains 344{,}720 quotes spanning 01/03/2022 to 11/19/2024, including bid/ask quotes, implied volatilities, dividend assumptions, spot and forward prices, and discount factors across strikes and expirations. The Kalshi data is structured as discrete, non-overlapping \$200 price buckets representing daily SPX year-end outcomes (13 buckets per year). Two calendar-alignment rules govern which quotes are used. First, an option is retained only if its expiration falls in the same calendar year as its quote date; the raw feed rolls to the following year's contract roughly two weeks before the current one settles, and without this filter the final trading days of the year would be priced off a contract with nearly a year to maturity rather than days. Second, because Kalshi trades continuously while SPXW options quote only on trading days, a new position is opened only on a calendar date where both markets have a genuine, contemporaneous quote; no stale or forward-projected option data is used to justify a trade. This is a deliberate design choice rather than a data-availability compromise: the strategy's premise is that the options market is the live, informationally current reference, which only holds while it is actually trading. On a day options are closed, a divergence between a frozen model estimate and a moving Kalshi price no longer indicates that Kalshi is mispriced --- it could equally mean the reverse. Existing positions continue to be marked to Kalshi's order book and settle normally on every calendar day regardless; only the opening of new positions is restricted to jointly-live days.

\subsection{Option Chain Cleaning}
Records missing bid, ask, \emph{and} implied volatility simultaneously are removed. Numeric columns are coerced to consistent types and dates standardized. Mid-prices are the average of bid and ask when both are present; if only one side is quoted, the other is assumed to be zero (giving a theoretical mid of half the quoted side), and such a quote is retained only if the Black--Scholes model price (at the vendor implied volatility) is within \$0.50 of that theoretical mid, otherwise it is discarded.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
  \begin{center}\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{moneyness_distribution.png}\end{center}
  \caption{Moneyness distribution}\label{fig:moneyness}
\end{wrapfigure}

A quote is deemed illiquid if its bid--ask spread exceeds 30\% of the mid-price, a generous threshold that primarily filters deep OTM/ITM options. Moneyness is defined as $K/F$ for calls and $2 - K/F$ for puts, where $K$ is the strike and $F$ the forward price, so the distribution centers at 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:moneyness}). Quotes more than two standard deviations from the mean moneyness are excluded as deep ITM/OTM.

Transformations scale implied volatilities from percent to decimal, convert time-to-maturity from days to years, and derive the implied continuous rate $r=-\ln(D)/T$ from the discount factor $D$. Spot prices are adjusted for discrete dividends as $S = S_0 - q\cdot D$, where $q$ is the total dollar dividend expected before expiry; this adjusted spot is used throughout, and the forward is $F = S\,e^{rT}$.

To secure a continuous volatility surface, implied volatilities missing from the vendor feed but having a valid mid-price are recovered by inverting the Black--Scholes formula via bisection. In our reproducible pipeline this reduces missing implied volatilities from 3{,}372 to 89, after which 310{,}525 of the original 344{,}720 quotes remain.

\subsection{Implied Volatility Smoothing}
For each quote date and option type, a least-squares univariate spline (LSQ) is fitted to implied volatility as a function of strike. Interior knots are placed at strike quantiles (guaranteeing the Schoenberg--Whitney condition for clustered strikes), and flat extrapolation is used outside the observed strike range to avoid unrealistic wings and negative volatilities. The smoothed series, ``LSQ\_Vol,'' is the basis for pricing and density estimation (Figure~\ref{fig:smoothing}).

Several alternatives were evaluated before selecting LSQ splines --- LOWESS, cubic splines, polynomial regression, PCHIP, and the SABR model --- and are documented separately; LSQ splines gave the best balance of smoothness, local flexibility, and stability without introducing arbitrage.

\subsection{Pricing Mechanism}
Options are priced with a spot-based Black--Scholes--Merton model on the dividend-adjusted spot $S$ with rate $r$ and volatility LSQ\_Vol:
\[ C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \quad P = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1),\quad
d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}},\; d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}. \]
To reduce bias from BSM's assumptions, a control-variate adjustment nudges the model toward the market mid, $\text{Price}_{adj} = \text{Price}_{BSM} + \lambda(\text{Mid} - \text{Price}_{BSM})$, with a fixed $\lambda = 0.2$. Against observed mids, the adjusted model achieves a mean absolute error of \$4.38 and RMSE of \$7.54 (Figure~\ref{fig:modelmkt}).

\section{Density Formation}
We construct benchmark estimates of the market-implied risk-neutral terminal distribution of SPX and discretize them to Kalshi's buckets. Two methods are carried forward --- the model-free Breeden--Litzenberger result and a GBM benchmark --- and a third heuristic (option price spreads) is described but discarded.

\subsection{Breeden--Litzenberger Risk-Neutral Density}
Under no-arbitrage, the risk-neutral density of the terminal underlying is recovered from the convexity of call prices in strike; dividing by the discount factor expresses it as a true (undiscounted) probability density:
\[ f_Q(K) = \frac{1}{DF}\,\frac{\partial^2 C(K)}{\partial K^2} = e^{rT}\,\frac{\partial^2 C(K)}{\partial K^2}. \]
We build the smoothed volatility curve from \emph{out-of-the-money} options (puts below the forward, calls above), which are less noisy than deep in-the-money quotes, using a least-squares spline with a modest number of knots (fewer than for pricing, since the second derivative amplifies any over-fitting of the smile). Synthetic BSM call prices are evaluated on a \emph{wide} strike grid (extended beyond the observed strikes with flat-volatility extrapolation) so the density has near-full support. Rather than differentiating naively --- which lets smile noise produce spurious multi-modality and negative densities --- we first impose the no-arbitrage shape on the price curve: the first derivative $\partial C/\partial K$ is constrained to lie in $[-DF, 0]$ and to be monotonically non-decreasing (an isotonic projection), so that $C(K)$ is convex and the recovered density is non-negative by construction, with no ad-hoc tail surgery. A light, mass-preserving smoothing removes the small kink introduced where flat extrapolation begins. Bucket probabilities are the trapezoidal integral $P([L,U]) = \int_L^U f_Q(K)\,dK$.

Crucially, the density is \emph{not} renormalized to integrate to one, and bucket probabilities are \emph{not} rescaled to sum to 100\%: the residual mass is the genuine probability that SPX breaches every predefined Kalshi bucket. The undiscounted $P([L,U])$ is compared directly to the Kalshi contract price, since the exchange's APY on positions already compensates for time value. On a representative date the recovered density integrates to $\approx 1$ on the wide grid without any rescaling and is centered on the forward (Figure~\ref{fig:density}).

\subsection{Parametric Benchmark via Geometric Brownian Motion}
As a computationally trivial benchmark we assume a lognormal terminal law,
\[ \log(S_T/S_0) \sim \mathcal N\!\left((r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T,\ \sigma^2 T\right), \qquad
P([L,U]) = \Phi(d(U)) - \Phi(d(L)),\quad d(K) = \frac{\ln(K/S_0) - (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \]
where $S_0$ is the dividend-adjusted \emph{spot}, $\sigma$ is the smoothed at-the-money implied volatility (strike nearest the forward), and $T$ is time to expiry. (The drift term $r$ implies a spot---not forward---anchor.) This benchmark captures location and scale but imposes symmetry and constant volatility, limiting its ability to capture skew and fat tails.

\subsection{Direct Estimation from Option Price Spreads (discarded)}
A heuristic estimating bucket probabilities from call/put price slopes across strikes, distance-weighted between calls and puts, was also tested. In practice it was unstable --- strike granularity and missing strikes produced erratic, poorly normalized PMFs --- and it was discarded from active strategy development, consistent with prior expectations.

\section{Strategy Considerations}
\textbf{Portfolio and benchmarking.} Performance metrics use daily returns annualized with a 252-day factor \emph{for both the strategy and the SPX benchmark}. A constant 3\% annual risk-free rate is used for Sharpe/alpha. Open positions are marked to the mid-price of a valid two-sided order book; a degenerate book (a zero bid against a 100-cent ask, which arises for buckets the index has left entirely) carries no tradable price and is marked to the last valid mid, then to the model. Execution uses market orders with Kalshi's fee schedule.

\textbf{Position sizing and risk control.} A lot size of 8 contracts is used, selected by a power-of-two hypertuning exercise balancing fee efficiency against liquidity. To keep risk bounded on the small capital base, \emph{exposure is capped at a single lot per bucket}: a repeat same-direction signal on a bucket already held is skipped, and an opposite signal closes the position. This ``no-pyramiding'' rule is the key difference from an unconstrained accumulation strategy, which --- while capable of large end-of-year settlement payoffs --- drives portfolio value through zero and renders return-based metrics ill-defined.

\textbf{Capital and fees.} The portfolio starts with \$200 of cash, providing a drawdown buffer given the capped exposure. All executions incorporate Kalshi's fee schedule, and the 3.75\% APY on cash and open positions is accrued monthly.

All thresholds and parameters above are centralized in the accompanying code (\texttt{config.py}) and documented in \texttt{ASSUMPTIONS.md}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Strategy Execution}
The strategy buys a bucket when the model probability exceeds the Kalshi ask (including fees) and sells when it is below the Kalshi bid (net of fees). Because trades must be fillable in the order book, we distinguish the theoretical portfolio (mark-to-model) from the executed portfolio (mark-to-market); their correlation ($\rho$) measures how closely the realizable strategy tracks the ideal one. The executed strategy is benchmarked against the SPX index (Figures~\ref{fig:strat_bench}, \ref{fig:strat_model}).

\textbf{Holding period and exit rule.} There is no fixed holding period, target spread, or time-based exit. The same entry test is evaluated daily on both sides, so a position, once opened, is held until either (i) the relationship reverses far enough to trigger the opposite-direction signal on that bucket --- not merely until the mispricing narrows to zero --- or (ii) the contract settles at year end. In practice this produced holding periods ranging from a single day to over six months in the same bucket within one year, and reversal-triggered exits were not always profitable: a position can be stopped out at a loss if the market moves against it before convergence completes. Repeated same-direction entry signals while a position is already at its one-lot cap do not generate new trades (Section~5); consequently the number of \emph{signals} evaluated each year (mispricing observed) substantially exceeds the number of trades actually \emph{executed} (fills that changed a position), and only the latter is a meaningful measure of turnover.

\subsection{Strategy Characteristics}
Under risk-managed sizing and strict same-day matching, the strategy is close to market-neutral: betas are within $\pm 0.04$ of zero and correlations to SPX are low, consistent with extracting mispricing rather than market direction. Drawdowns are modest across all years (under $5\%$). The two methods are comparable rather than one dominating: GBM attains the single highest Sharpe ratio (1.41 in 2022), but Breeden--Litzenberger is the only method with a positive Sharpe in every year, including 2024 (0.12 versus GBM's $-0.61$) --- consistent with BL's theoretical advantage in capturing skew when the terminal distribution departs from lognormal, which matters most in the year GBM's constant-volatility assumption is most strained.

A note on marking is important here. Late in each year, once the index has moved past a bucket, that bucket's order book becomes one-sided --- a zero bid against a 100-cent ask --- because no participant will offer a contract that is already worthless. Treating that 100-cent ask as a liquidation price marks short positions at a spurious catastrophic loss; correcting the marking (as described under Strategy Considerations) removes what would otherwise appear as a large but entirely artificial 2024 drawdown. A residual caveat remains for 2024 specifically: the option feed ends 11/19/2024, and SPX closed above every predefined bucket, so the economically relevant contract that year was the open-ended tail bucket, which is outside the present dataset.

Mark-to-model versus mark-to-market correlations range from $\rho \approx 0$ to $0.6$ across year/model combinations, quantifying how much of the theoretically identified edge survives execution and liquidity --- a central practical limitation of trading these markets. Restricting new trades to days when both markets are genuinely live (Section~3) removes a source of divergence between the two series that would otherwise be conflated with execution slippage.

\subsection{Cross-Market Corroboration via Iron-Condor Replication}
As an independent, model-free check we replicate each Kalshi bucket $[L,U]$ with an SPX call condor whose payoff is one inside the bucket and zero outside (two tight call spreads). Comparing the condor's mid-price to the Kalshi price across all tradable bucket-days, the average replication error is only $\sim$2--3 cents (basis risk concentrated at the binary's knife-edge), while roughly \textbf{24\%} of quotes show a cross-market dislocation larger than five cents (Figure~\ref{fig:hedge}). This confirms, without any density model, that persistent tradable discrepancies exist between the two markets.

\section{Conclusion}
This study explores statistical arbitrage between Kalshi event contracts and SPX options by constructing a risk-neutral density from the liquid options market and comparing it to Kalshi's order-book prices. Under a realistic, risk-controlled implementation the option-implied signals produce small but generally positive, low-drawdown, near market-neutral returns, and an independent iron-condor replication corroborates that a meaningful fraction of Kalshi quotes are dislocated from the options market. The headline conclusion is qualified: the edge is real but modest once position sizing is constrained to keep risk bounded.

The dominant practical obstacle is liquidity. Wide spreads and thin books make execution timing critical and cap scalability, and the mark-to-model/mark-to-market correlation --- well below 1 in every case --- shows how much theoretical edge is lost in practice. Refinement of the RND (volatility surface, discounting conventions) and richer execution modeling --- fee-free limit orders, bid/ask-size-aware sizing --- are the clearest paths to improvement.

The natural next step, left as future work, is a fully \emph{hedged} strategy that pairs each Kalshi position with its offsetting SPX iron condor and tracks both legs' mark-to-market daily, isolating the cross-market spread from directional risk. Extending beyond year-end SPX buckets to shorter horizons and other Kalshi products (Bitcoin, crude oil) is also promising. As Kalshi's liquidity and product breadth grow, the feasibility of these approaches should improve.
